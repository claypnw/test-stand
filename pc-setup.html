Set up the house cameras on your PC

This page walks you through putting the camera viewer on your Windows PC. One folder, three small files, and a few settings. Nothing here changes anything else on your PC, and you can stop the whole thing at any time by closing one window (step 8).

One honest word first: this setup has not been tried on your unit yet. Your cameras and your unit are at your house, on your home network, and no one can reach them from outside the house — so the first real try happens at your house, with you there. The address forms in step 7 are the ones your unit's family is known to use, but each one is a candidate, not a promise. Trying them in order is how the right one gets found.

Step 1 — Make one folder

  1. On your PC desktop, right-click an empty spot.
  2. Pick New, then Folder.
  3. Name the folder Cameras.

Every file in this setup goes in that one folder.

Step 2 — Get the translator program

The viewer page is a web page, and web pages cannot take a camera picture straight from your unit. A small free program does that middle job: it takes the pictures from the unit and hands them to the web page. The program is called go2rtc. It costs nothing and needs no account.

  1. Open the maker's download page: go2rtc releases page on GitHub.
  2. On that page, find the newest release at the top, and in its list of files pick the Windows one: go2rtc_win64.zip (that is the build for a Windows PC like yours).
  3. Save the file, then open it and pull out the program inside, go2rtc.exe.
  4. Put go2rtc.exe in your Cameras folder.

Step 3 — Put the two package files in the same folder

These two files were made for your cameras. Save each one into the same Cameras folder: right-click the link, pick Save link as…, and choose the Cameras folder.

go2rtc.yaml — the settings file. It holds the ten camera stream lines the translator will use.
start-cameras.bat — the start file. You will double-click this one to start everything. It is a plain word file: you can open it in Notepad and read every word in it, and all it does is start the translator and open the camera page.

Your Cameras folder should now hold three files: go2rtc.exe, go2rtc.yaml, and start-cameras.bat.

Step 4 — Find the unit's address

Your unit (the box your ten cameras talk to) has an address on your home network. It looks like numbers with dots, for example 192.168.1.120. Find the real one like this:

  1. Go to the unit and look at its own screen (the monitor hooked to the unit).
  2. Open the unit's menu and find the page called Fast Network.
  3. The address is on that page, marked as the IP address. Write it down exactly.

Do not use 192.168.1.205. That address showed up in alert mail from March 2024. It is old, and the unit's address may have changed since. Only the address on the unit's screen today counts.

Step 5 — Write the address and login into the settings file

  1. Open the settings file in Notepad, this way:
    1. Open Notepad by itself first: click the Start search box, type Notepad, and click it.
    2. In Notepad, click File, then Open.
    3. Go to the Cameras folder on the C drive. The list will look empty at first. At the bottom of the Open window, change the file-type box from Text Documents to All Files, and go2rtc.yaml will appear.
    4. Click go2rtc.yaml, then click Open.
  2. Use Edit > Replace three times:
    • Replace UNIT-ADDRESS with the address you wrote down in step 4.
    • Replace UNIT-USER with the unit's login name.
    • Replace UNIT-PASS with the unit's password.
    Pick Replace All each time, so all ten camera lines get changed at once.
  3. Save the file and close Notepad.

About the login: the unit ships with the login name admin and a blank password. If you never changed them, use admin, and for the password just erase the words UNIT-PASS and leave the rest of the line as it is — the line will read rtsp://admin:@… and that is right. If you did set a login or password on the unit, use yours.

Step 6 — Start it

  1. Double-click start-cameras.bat in the Cameras folder.
  2. A black window opens. That is the translator running. Leave that window open the whole time you watch the cameras.
  3. Chrome opens the house camera page on its own.

Step 7 — What you should see, and what to try if you don't

You should see the camera page with a real, moving picture in each square — your ten cameras, live. Tap a picture to make it big; tap it again to go back.

The page also has a Sound: Off / On button in the top bar. Tap it on, make one camera big, and that camera's sound plays — what its microphone hears. In the grid the sound stays off, so ten microphones do not all play at once.

One plain word on talking back: with one camera big, a Hold to talk button appears, wired so holding it should let you speak out of that camera's small speaker. That part is a try at the house, not a promise — whether your unit passes talk-back this way is one of the things the first test settles.

If the squares stay black, work down this list:

  1. Check the black translator window is still open. If it was closed, double-click start-cameras.bat again.
  2. Check the address from step 4 was written into the settings file exactly, dots and all, and that the login is right.
  3. Try the next address form. The settings file now uses the first form. The other forms your unit's family is known to use are below, in order. Each one is a candidate — none is proven on your unit yet. To try one: change the ten stream lines in go2rtc.yaml to the new form (same address and login, only the part after the address changes), save, close the translator window, and double-click start-cameras.bat again.

The form already in the file (form 1):

rtsp://admin:PASSWORD@ADDRESS:554/ch0/main … ch9/main

Form 2 — channels counted from 1, in the last digits (101 is camera 1, 201 is camera 2, up to 1001 for camera 10):

rtsp://admin:PASSWORD@ADDRESS:554/Streaming/Channels/101

Form 3 — a short two-digit form (11, 12, … or 01, 02, …):

rtsp://admin:PASSWORD@ADDRESS:554/11

Form 4 — the first form, but on port 80 instead of 554:

rtsp://admin:PASSWORD@ADDRESS:80/ch0/main … ch9/main

Last road: your unit may also speak a standard called ONVIF — a way for programs to ask the unit itself what its stream addresses are. A small free program named ONVIF Device Manager can read the true addresses straight off the unit, which settles the question in one step. If you get this far and still have black squares, stop there; that road is easier with a walk-through than with a written page.

Step 8 — How to stop it

Close the black translator window. That stops the sending, and the camera page will have no pictures until you start again with start-cameras.bat. Closing the Chrome tab is fine at any time.
